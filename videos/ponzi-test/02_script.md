# Ponzi: Fifty Percent in Ninety Days

## Part 1: The line

Boston, July 1920. A line stretches more than half a mile. In three days, $1.7 million leaves one man's bank account. Where was it all coming from?

## Part 2: A coupon from Spain

In August 1919, Italian immigrant Charles Ponzi found an international reply coupon in a letter from Spain. He claimed he could buy the coupons cheaply in Europe and redeem them in America for more. His notes promised fifty percent in ninety days. He usually paid in forty-five.

## Part 3: Cash to the ceiling

He began in December 1919 with $150. In June 1920 alone, 7,824 people paid in about $2.5 million. Sixteen clerks stacked cash in closets to the ceiling. Even his lawyer, a juvenile court judge, invested $5,400.

On 24 July, he made the Boston Post's front page. The day before, he had hired a publicist.

## Part 4: A winding staircase

In the Post, financial publisher Clarence Barron was blunt: "No man of wide financial or investment experience would look twice." His biographer, Mitchell Zuckoff, notes there were never enough coupons in the world to carry that much money. His publicist, William McMasters, told the Post's acting publisher that Ponzi was "as crooked as a winding staircase."

## Part 5: The run

On 26 July, after a three-hour grilling, Ponzi agreed to stop taking money. That day, $572,000 left his account at Hanover Trust. Then $228,000. Then $905,000.

On 2 August, the Post printed McMasters's verdict: Ponzi was hopelessly insolvent. On 9 August, his account was $331,000 overdrawn, and a creditor filed a bankruptcy petition. Two days later, the Post revealed he had served jail time in Montreal for forgery, under the name Ponsi.

Ponzi told reporter Herbert Baldwin: "You did a fine job on me." Within days, he was under arrest.

## Part 6: The bill

He pleaded guilty to federal mail fraud and got five years. In 1925, a state jury convicted him of larceny. Even investors paid out in the final days had to give it back.

Noteholders recovered, by different estimates, twelve to thirty-seven and a half cents on the dollar. Ponzi died in a Rio charity ward in January 1949.

## Part 7: Whose money

The signs were there. A return no real trade could produce. A founder who sued the newspaper asking questions. And early payouts, which proved only that new money was arriving.

The question was never whether it paid. It was who was paying.
