## Summary
Between December 1919 and August 1920, Charles Ponzi, an Italian immigrant with prison records in Montreal and Atlanta, ran the "Securities Exchange Company" from 27 School Street in Boston [1][4][6][10]. He borrowed money from the public on promissory notes. The notes promised 50% on top of the principal in 90 days, but he usually paid in 45 days [4]. He said the profit came from buying international postal reply coupons cheaply abroad and redeeming them in the US [1][6]. The US Supreme Court later found that he "made no investments of any kind" and was "always insolvent" [1]. Court records put the money taken in at $9,582,591.82, against notes promising $14,374,755.59 [4]. One account counts 30,195 investors [11]. Many popular sources say about $15 million [6][7][10]. The Boston Post's reporting started a run on the company in late July 1920 [1][14]. A creditor's bankruptcy petition followed on 9 August 1920 [4]. On 11 August the Post revealed his Montreal prison record [9], and he was arrested on 12 or 13 August [11][7]. Ponzi pleaded guilty to federal mail fraud (five years) [2]. He was later convicted of state larceny (seven to nine years) [3]. He was deported to Italy in 1934 [11][21] and died in a charity ward in Rio de Janeiro in January 1949 [11][13]. Noteholders got back somewhere between 12 cents [11] and 37.5 cents [12] on the dollar. The two sources measure this differently.

## Timeline
- **1882**: Ponzi is born in Italy. American Heritage and Boston.com say Parma; the ABA Journal says he came "from Lugo" [11][17][10].
- **November 1903**: He arrives in America [11]. The ABA Journal says he landed in Boston aged 21 with $2.50 and no English [10].
- **1908–1910**: He is jailed in Canada for forging cheques [6]. American Heritage gives the cheque as $423.68 and the jail as St. Vincent de Paul, Montreal, where he was held under the name "Ponsi" [11].
- **1910–1912**: He is held at the Atlanta Federal Penitentiary for bringing five Italians illegally into the US from Canada [6].
- **4 February 1918**: He marries Rose Marie Gnecco (spelled "Guecco" in American Heritage) of Somerville [11][17].
- **August 1919**: A letter from Spain contains an international reply coupon, which gives him the idea [11].
- **December 1919**: He starts with $150 in capital [1][4]. Fifteen investors lend $870 [11].
- **February 1920**: Police and postal inspectors begin investigating [10].
- **February–April 1920**: Monthly intake grows: $5,290 in February, $28,724 in March, and $141,671 from 471 people in April [11].
- **June 1920**: 7,824 people pay in about $2.5 million [11].
- **Mid-July 1920**: Weekly receipts approach $1,000,000, and the operation reaches "over most of New England and beyond" [4].
- **19–24 July 1920**: Ponzi's balance at Hanover Trust rises from $334,000 to $871,000 [1].
- **23 July 1920**: Ponzi hires publicist William H. McMasters [14].
- **24 July 1920 (Saturday)**: A favourable front-page profile runs in the Boston Post. Secondary sources give its headline as "DOUBLES THE MONEY WITHIN THREE MONTHS" [18][14].
- **26 July 1920**: Ponzi stops taking money [4]. After about three hours of questioning by DA Pelletier, US Attorney Gallagher and Attorney General J. Weston Allen, it is announced that he will take no new money until an audit is done [14][11]. $572,000 is withdrawn from Hanover Trust that day [1].
- **27–28 July 1920**: Hanover withdrawals of $228,000 and $905,000 [1].
- **2 August 1920**: The Post publishes McMasters's exposé, "Declares Ponzi Is Now Hopelessly Insolvent" [14]. Taft's opinion describes the resulting "wild scramble" [1].
- **3 August 1920**: A second McMasters article appears [14].
- **8 August 1920**: Post reporter P.A. Santosuosso interviews Ponzi at his home [9].
- **9 August 1920**: Ponzi's Hanover account is overdrawn by $331,000 [1]. A creditor's bankruptcy petition is filed in the US District Court in Massachusetts [4]. The First Circuit treats this as the end of his "career" [4].
- **11 August 1920**: The Post runs "CANADIAN 'PONSI' SERVED JAIL TERM" [9]. A 1920 wire report says Commissioner Allen closed Hanover Trust "Wednesday", which would be 11 August. One database gives the suspension as 12 August [26][25].
- **10 August 1920**: The last withdrawals are counted. Between 19 July and 10 August, $6,692,000 is withdrawn from Hanover [1].
- **12 or 13 August 1920**: Ponzi is arrested. American Heritage says a US marshal took him on 13 August at his Lexington home [11]. Other accounts say 12 August [7].
- **11 September 1920**: A Suffolk County grand jury returns 22 indictments: 11 for larceny, 10 for being an accessory before the fact, and 1 for conspiracy [3].
- **1 October 1920**: Two federal mail-fraud indictments [2], with 43 counts each according to the ABA Journal [10].
- **25 October 1920**: He is adjudged bankrupt [4].
- **30 November 1920**: He pleads guilty to the first count of one federal indictment and is sentenced to five years in the Plymouth House of Correction [2]. The USPIS gives 1 November and "86 counts" [7].
- **1921**: The Post wins the Pulitzer Prize for Public Service [8][9].
- **21 April 1921**: A Massachusetts court issues a writ to bring Ponzi from federal custody to state trial [2].
- **27 March 1922**: In Ponzi v. Fessenden, the Supreme Court rules unanimously that the state can try him [2][10].
- **October 1922 to 1 December 1922**: First state trial, on 12 indictments. He is found not guilty [3].
- **November 1924**: Second state trial. The jury disagrees on some indictments and a directed not-guilty verdict is entered on another [3].
- **28 April 1924**: In Cunningham v. Brown, the Supreme Court holds that investors who were paid out in the final days must return the money as unlawful preferences [1].
- **26 February 1925**: Third trial. Guilty on all counts. He is sentenced as a "common and notorious thief" to seven to nine years [3].
- **February 1926**: A Duval County, Florida grand jury indicts him over the "Charpon Land Syndicate" [24].
- **28 May 1926**: The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court rejects his appeal [3].
- **3 June 1926**: He jumps bail in Florida. On 28 June 1926 he is captured at New Orleans [11].
- **February 1927**: He begins his state term at Charlestown State Prison [11].
- **5 January 1931**: TIME reports a creditor dividend bringing total payments to 37.5% [12].
- **12 July 1934**: He surrenders at the East Boston immigration station, according to a press-photo caption [22].
- **30 July 1934**: Ponzi v. Ward, his habeas petition against the Commissioner of Immigration, is decided in D. Mass. [5].
- **7 October 1934**: He is deported from Boston aboard the Vulcania [11][21].
- **1939–1942**: He works as a branch manager in Rio de Janeiro for an Italian airline, named as LATI by TIME [11][13].
- **January 1949**: He dies in a charity ward in Rio [11][13]. The day is usually given as 18 January (see Disputed).
- **1968**: William McMasters dies [14].
- **2009**: The New York Times reports the discovery of McMasters's lost memoir [15][14].
- **Today (23 November 2026)**: Every principal is dead, and no litigation is open. The case lives on in the term "Ponzi scheme" and in Cunningham v. Brown, the 1924 Supreme Court ruling that ordered last-minute withdrawals returned [1].

## People
- **Charles Ponzi**: founder of the Securities Exchange Company. He pleaded guilty to one count of using the mails in a scheme to defraud and got five years in the Plymouth House of Correction [2]. In Massachusetts he was acquitted in 1922, had a hung jury in 1924, and was convicted of larceny in 1925 and sentenced to seven to nine years as a "common and notorious thief" [3]. He was indicted in Florida in 1926 and jumped bail [24][11]. He was deported in 1934 and died in 1949 [11][13].
- **Rose Gnecco Ponzi**: his wife [17][11]. The sources show no charges. American Heritage says she divorced him and never remarried [11].
- **Imelda Ponzi**: his mother, brought over from Italy [11]. Biographer Zuckoff says this was done to calm fears that he would run [16].
- **Louis Casullo and John Dondero**: silent partners who lent him early money. American Heritage says Casullo fled to Italy with more than $1 million and Dondero disappeared with "a trunkful of money" [11]. I found no legal outcome for either.
- **Richard Grozier**: acting publisher of the Boston Post. He pushed the investigation and paid McMasters for his story [9][14][11]. Not charged.
- **Edward J. Dunn**: city editor of the Post. He assigned the reporters [9].
- **P.A. Santosuosso and Herbert L. Baldwin**: Post reporters. Baldwin confirmed the Montreal record [9]. American Heritage gives Baldwin's middle initial as "E." [11].
- **Clarence W. Barron**: owner of the Boston News Bureau and linked to Dow Jones and the WSJ. He was the Post's financial sceptic [9][11].
- **William H. McMasters**: Ponzi's publicist, who turned on him and wrote the 2 August exposé. Not charged. He died in 1968 and was honoured by the ACFE in 2011 [14].
- **Frank Leveroni**: Boston juvenile court judge and Ponzi's lawyer. He invested $5,400 [11].
- **Daniel H. Coakley**: lawyer, reportedly paid a $100,000 fee [11].
- **Henry Chmielinski**: president of Hanover Trust, "ruined" when the bank failed [11]. I found no record of charges against him.
- **William S. McNary**: treasurer of Hanover Trust, who arranged the McMasters meeting [14].
- **Joseph C. Allen**: Massachusetts Commissioner of Banks, who closed Hanover Trust [11][26].
- **Joseph C. Pelletier**: Suffolk County District Attorney [11][14].
- **Daniel J. Gallagher**: US Attorney for Massachusetts [14].
- **J. Weston Allen**: Massachusetts Attorney General [14].
- **Edwin U. Curtis**: Boston police commissioner, who sent inspectors [11].
- **Edwin Pride**: government auditor of Ponzi's books. He appears only in secondary sources [19].
- **"Bodenstab"**: the investor named in the indictment that produced the 1925 conviction. The alleged larcenies were $1,000 on 8 July, $600 on 16 July and $400 on 20 July 1920 [3].
- **Richard Engstrom**: sold Ponzi the Lexington house and invested $20,000 [11].
- **Chief Justice William Howard Taft**: wrote both Ponzi v. Fessenden and Cunningham v. Brown [2][1].
- **Justice Pierce (Massachusetts SJC)**: wrote Commonwealth v. Ponzi [3].
- **Governor Joseph B. Ely**: refused Ponzi's requests for clemency [11].
- **Judge Clarence Hale**: named as the federal sentencing judge only in secondary material I could not verify (see Disputed).

## Key numbers
- **$150**: Ponzi's starting capital in December 1919. Source: Supreme Court and First Circuit [1][4].
- **50% in 90 days, usually paid in 45**: the note terms, per the First Circuit [4]. Taft writes "$150 for every $100 loaned" in 90 days [1]. American Heritage says "50% in 45 days; 100% in 90 days", which conflicts [11]. TIME (1931) says 50% in 45 days [12].
- **$9,582,591.82**: cash taken in from December 1919 to 26 July 1920. Source: the First Circuit [4]. The Supreme Court rounds it to $9,582,000 [1], and American Heritage gives $9,582,591 [11].
- **$14,374,755.59**: what Ponzi had promised to repay on those notes [4]. Variants are $14,374,000 [1] and $14,374,818 [11].
- **About $15 million**: the headline figure in the National Archives, ABA Journal, USPIS and Smithsonian [6][10][7][19]. My reading: it is close to the promised-return figure, not the cash received. Other outlets say about $10 million [14][17].
- **Investors**: 30,195 [11], about 30,000 [14], or about 20,000 per the NYT as cited by Boston.com [17].
- **About $300**: the average investment [11].
- **10%**: commission paid to agents [1].
- **$4,263,652.14**: unsecured notes still outstanding at bankruptcy [4].
- **Liabilities over $7 million, assets under $4 million**: American Heritage [11]. The Smithsonian reports the auditor first found a $3 million deficit and later revised it to $7 million (search excerpt only) [19].
- **Hanover Trust balances**: $334,000 at the open on 19 July and $871,000 at the close on 24 July. Withdrawals were $572,000 on 26 July, $228,000 on 27 July and $905,000 on 28 July. There was a $331,000 overdraft on 9 August, and $6,692,000 was withdrawn from 19 July to 10 August. All from the Supreme Court [1].
- **Daily intake**: up to $250,000 a day [10][7][17]. The ACFE says "over $3 million in one day" after the 24 July story [14], which does not fit with the court's figure of about $1 million a week [4]. Treat the ACFE figure as doubtful.
- **$500,000**: taken in during May 1920, per the ACFE [14]. American Heritage's monthly numbers are different [11].
- **$61**: the value of reply coupons found in the final audit. This comes from Donald Dunn's book *Ponzi!*, quoted by the Smithsonian Postal Museum (search excerpt only) [20].
- **Recovery for victims**: 12 cents on the dollar [11], or 37.5% in total by January 1931 [12]. Unresolved, and they may measure different bases.
- **$5 million**: Ponzi's libel suit against the Post [14].
- **$6,000**: what Grozier paid McMasters [14].
- **Sentences**: five years federal [2] and seven to nine years state [3]. TIME also says seven to nine [12].
- **$2.50**: the cash Ponzi says he had when he arrived [10][17].

## How the scheme worked
- **The cover story**: A reply coupon was created by the Universal Postal Union in 1906 [20]. The Massachusetts court admitted the 1906 postal treaty as evidence [3]. It let a sender prepay a reply. After the First World War, currencies in Europe collapsed while coupon exchange rates did not keep up [20]. Ponzi claimed he bought coupons cheaply in weak-currency countries and redeemed them in the US for more [6][1]. Zuckoff says it worked on paper, but not at scale: there were too few coupons, and the costs of handling them would wipe out the margin [16]. Postal inspectors found that worldwide coupon sales were far too small to support Ponzi's claims [20].
- **Where the money came from**: Investors bought notes promising 50% [4]. Agents earned 10% commissions to bring people in [1]. Many of the first investors were immigrants who already used the coupons to write home [16].
- **Where it went**: Taft found that Ponzi "made no investments of any kind" and paid each note from later investors' money [1]. The USPIS says he never found a way to turn coupons into cash [7]. After the exposé, the postal service reported that he had never bought or redeemed coupons [14]. Paying early, at 45 days, "stimulated their avidity" [1]. Money was also spent on the Lexington house, a chauffeured Locomobile and lawyers [11]. Partners Casullo and Dondero allegedly vanished with large sums [11].
- **How it was hidden and kept alive**: He deposited heavily with Hanover Trust and became one of its directors [11][1]. He bought public trust with paid publicity [14] and with displays of solvency: during the July run he paid out on demand [14]. He moved his mother and wife into a bought home to show he was not leaving [16]. He threatened critics and sued the Post for $5 million [9][14]. His criminal past under the name "Ponsi" stayed hidden until 11 August [9][6]. In Taft's words, "He was always insolvent, and became daily more so, the more his business succeeded" [1].

## Scenes
1. **The letter from Spain, August 1919.** A reply coupon falls out of a piece of business mail. Ponzi later said: "I thought about its value on this side of the Atlantic and its value on the other side." [11]
2. **The back room, December 1919.** He has $150 in capital [1] and a small back room at the Tremont Trust Company on School Street [11]. Fifteen people lend $870 [11].
3. **The line on School Street, June 1920.** From City Hall's high oval windows, a line of straw hats looks like a "roiled, wheat-colored stream". Inside, there is free coffee and doughnuts. Pregnant and elderly women are let in through a side door [11]. The queue runs from the City Hall Annex, through City Hall Avenue and up the stairs [search excerpt, see 11].
4. **The morning arrival.** A Japanese chauffeur drives him in from Lexington in a cream-coloured Locomobile. Police clear the way, and office girls blow kisses. He stands about 5'3", with a pearl stickpin, a gold-headed Malacca cane and a Turkish cigarette in an ivory-and-gold holder. Sixteen clerks stack cash in closets up to the ceiling [11].
5. **The publicist's first meeting, 23 July 1920,** in Judge Leveroni's office [14]. McMasters later wrote: "I was not averse to having a millionaire for a client, especially one who evidently wanted to spend money lavishly" [14].
6. **The three-hour grilling, 26 July 1920.** Ponzi faces the DA, the US Attorney and the Attorney General. The Attorney General says he will be "the greatest Italian whoever came to America". Ponzi replies: "Don't forget Columbus, Mr. Allen!" [14]. He agrees to stop taking money [4][11].
7. **The run, 26–28 July.** The line stretches more than half a mile. Investors weep and some faint, and Ponzi keeps paying [14]. Over three days, $1.7 million leaves Hanover Trust [1].
8. **"Three groans for the Post!"** After the exposé, a crowd on Washington Street cheers Ponzi and he turns the cheer on the newspaper [11]. He warns Baldwin that "you are going to get the presses ripped out of your building" [9].
9. **The Montreal photograph, 8–11 August.** After Santosuosso's interview at Lexington, a Montreal correspondent sends a tip about a "Ponsi". Baldwin travels north, and sources there identify Ponzi from a photo [9]. The Post's city editor also telegraphs the Atlanta warden; the telegram survives in the National Archives [6]. The headline on 11 August reads "CANADIAN 'PONSI' SERVED JAIL TERM". Afterwards Ponzi tells Baldwin: "You did a fine job on me." [9]
10. **The overdraft, 9 August.** The account is $331,000 overdrawn and creditors file for bankruptcy [1][4]. Within days the bank commissioner shuts Hanover Trust [26][11].
11. **The arrest at Lexington, 12 or 13 August.** A US marshal takes him at his home [11]. The house was at 19 Slocum Road [17].
12. **The Vulcania, 7 October 1934.** He boards in steerage with 23 other deportees [21] and gives a Fascist salute on the way up [11]. Leslie Jones photos show him on the gangplank and guarded by police in a hotel [23].
13. **The Rio ward, January 1949.** He is half blind and partly paralysed, sharing a room with an old man with a hacking cough [11][13].

## Outcome and status today
- **Federal**: guilty plea on 30 November 1920 to one count of using the mails to defraud. Five years in the Plymouth House of Correction [2].
- **State**: acquitted on 1 December 1922, hung jury in November 1924, convicted on 26 February 1925. Seven to nine years as a common and notorious thief. Appeal dismissed on 28 May 1926 [3].
- **Constitutional rulings**: The Supreme Court allowed the state to try him while he was in federal custody [2]. The SJC rejected his double-jeopardy argument [3].
- **Florida**: indicted in 1926 over a land syndicate. He jumped bail, was caught at New Orleans and returned to Massachusetts [24][11][12].
- **Deportation**: he lost his habeas petition in July 1934 [5] and was deported on 7 October 1934 [11][21].
- **Bankruptcy and victims**: He was adjudged bankrupt on 25 October 1920 [4]. Cunningham v. Brown (1924) made investors who withdrew in the final weeks return the money and share equally with the others [1]. Reported recovery: 12 cents on the dollar [11], or 37.5% by 1931 [12]. Several Boston trust companies fell with him [12], including Hanover Trust [11][26].
- **The press**: the Boston Post won the 1921 Pulitzer for Public Service [8][9].
- **Status today**: Ponzi died in January 1949 in Rio [11][13]. McMasters died in 1968 [14]. All other named figures were adults in 1920 and are long dead; the sources give no dates for most of them. No case is open.

## Disputed or uncertain
- **Total taken**: the court figure is $9.58 million [4]. Popular accounts say $15 million [6][7][10] or $10 million [14][17].
- **Federal plea date and counts**: the 1922 Supreme Court record says 30 November 1920, first count of one indictment [2]. The USPIS says 1 November, "86 counts" [7]. Go with the court record.
- **ABA Journal headline**: it says he "pleads guilty to larceny" [10]. The 30 November plea was federal mail fraud [2]. The larceny conviction came in 1925 after a jury trial [3].
- **Year of the state conviction**: 1925 [3][11]. TIME (1931) says 1924 [12].
- **Arrest date**: 12 or 13 August 1920, and whether he was arrested or surrendered [11][7].
- **Hanover Trust closure date**: 11 or 12 August [26][25].
- **Birthplace**: Parma [11][17] or Lugo [10].
- **Reporter's name**: Herbert L. Baldwin [9] or Herbert E. Baldwin [11].
- **Note terms**: "50% in 45 days, 100% in 90 days" [11], against 50% in 90 days paid early at 45 days [4][1].
- **Recovery rate**: 12% [11] or 37.5% [12].
- **Auditor's deficit**: $3 million revised to $7 million [19].
- **The "$61 in coupons" detail**: it rests on one book, cited second-hand [20].
- **Judge Clarence Hale**: his name and the sentencing quote come only from a blog quoting Wikipedia. Not verified.
- **Exact death date and the $75 estate**: 18 January 1949 and the $75 estate appear only in secondary sources. TIME and American Heritage confirm only that he died in a Rio charity ward in January 1949 [13][11].
- **The "best show… since the landing of the Pilgrims" line**: often attributed to Ponzi's last interview, but it has no traceable primary source [18]. Do not use it on air without the original.
- **TIME error**: TIME (1931) places the office on "Hanover Street" [12]. The office was at 27 School Street [10][11].
- **Allegations against others**: the claims about Casullo, Dondero and Hanover's officers come from American Heritage [11]. I found no charges or verdicts against them, so phrase these as allegations.
- **Access limits**: several sources (marked in the Sources list) blocked direct access and were checked only through search excerpts. Read them in the original before scripting from them.

## Warning signs
1. **The promised return was absurd.** 50% in 45–90 days. Barron said no experienced investor "would look twice" [9].
2. **The trade was too small for the money.** Worldwide coupon sales could not support the volume Ponzi claimed [20][16]. If a strategy cannot absorb the money being raised, the money is going somewhere else.
3. **He paid early.** Early, generous payouts built trust and "stimulated their avidity" [1]. A reliable payout record is not proof of profit.
4. **Commissions drove recruitment.** Agents earned 10% for bringing in money [1].
5. **The founder had a hidden record.** Forgery and smuggling convictions, under a different spelling of his name [6][9].
6. **He controlled his own bank.** He became a director of the bank that held the money [11].
7. **He attacked his critics.** Threats against the Post and a $5 million lawsuit [9][14].
8. **Publicity stood in for evidence.** He hired a publicist and placed glowing coverage [14].
9. **He paid out during the run.** Paying everyone during a run looked like strength, but it emptied the bank account [1][14].
10. **Officials had warned people.** Postal inspectors told the public to stay away, and many ignored the warnings [7].
11. **He sold to people who trusted him.** His first investors came from immigrant networks that already used the coupons. Even reporters invested [16].
